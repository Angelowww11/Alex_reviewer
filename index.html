Midterm Reviewer: Statistical Analysis and Analytics Modeling

How to use this reviewer

This reviewer teaches the full set of concepts covered by Modules 1–5. The ten questions at the end of each module are a practice set; they do not imply that the exam will cover only ten concepts. Learn the definitions, distinctions, formulas, assumptions, and worked examples throughout each module. Try each practice set before checking the answer key.

Formula and notation guide

  • Population: the complete group of interest. Sample: the observed subset used to learn about that group.
  • Σ means “sum.” n is the number of sample observations; N is population size.
  • Actual is the observed value; forecast/prediction is the model’s estimate. A residual/error is actual minus predicted.
  • Results must retain meaningful units. A slope is measured in outcome units per predictor unit.

Module 1 - Exploratory Data Analysis and Descriptive Statistics

1. Why summarize and explore data?

Raw data are observations before they have been organized or analyzed, such as unfiltered survey responses, exam scores, sensor readings, or server logs. A data set is a collection of observations on one or more variables. A variable is a characteristic that can take different values across cases; an observation (or measurement) is one recorded value. In a customer table, customer ID, age, and address are variables; one customer's row is an observation.

Statistics is the process of collecting, organizing, analyzing, presenting, and interpreting data to support decisions. Descriptive statistics summarize the data actually observed, using tables, graphs, and numerical measures. Inferential statistics use sample data to estimate, test, or predict something about a wider population. For example, a poll of selected customers describes the respondents; using it to estimate all customers' preferences is inference and carries sampling uncertainty.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is the initial investigation of data with summaries and visualizations. It helps reveal patterns and relationships, detect unusual observations, find missing or inconsistent values, check assumptions, and guide later modeling and preprocessing. EDA is not merely making charts: it is asking whether the data are plausible and what analytical methods fit them. An outlier is a signal to investigate, not automatically a value to delete.

2. Types of variables

First distinguish numerical measurements from categories:

  • Quantitative (numerical) variables express amounts. A discrete variable has countable values, usually whole-number counts (e.g., students or defects). A continuous variable can take any value in an interval, subject to measurement precision (e.g., height, weight, elapsed time).
  • Qualitative (categorical) variables classify cases. Nominal categories have no natural order (e.g., department or color). Ordinal categories have a meaningful order but gaps between levels are not necessarily equal (e.g., education level, satisfaction rating, finish place). Binary/dichotomous variables have two categories (e.g., yes/no, true/false, 0/1); binary is a special case of categorical data.

Do not confuse a number used as a label (such as a student ID or postal code) with a measured quantity. Arithmetic on identifiers has no meaningful interpretation. Also distinguish discrete from ordinal: counts are numerical quantities, while ordered labels are categories.

3. Center: mean, median, and mode

Measures of central tendency describe a typical or central value. Select a measure that fits both the variable type and the distribution.

  • Arithmetic mean: add values and divide by the number of values. For ungrouped observations:
    \[\bar{x}=\frac{\sum x_i}{n}\]
    The mean uses every value, but extreme values can pull it toward the tail.
  • Median: sort the data and take the middle. For odd n it is the central ranked observation; for even n it is the mean of the two central observations. It is resistant to outliers and useful for skewed numerical data.
  • Mode: the most frequent value/category. A data set may have one mode, multiple tied modes, or no uniquely useful mode. It works for categorical data as well as numbers.

Example: for 2, 3, 3, 4, 18, the mean is 6, median is 3, and mode is 3. The large value 18 moves the mean more than the median. For a roughly symmetric distribution, mean and median are often close. For a right-skewed distribution, the long high-value tail tends to pull the mean above the median; for a left-skewed distribution, the long low-value tail tends to pull it below the median. The mode is often near the peak, but exact ordering can vary with the distribution.

Grouped and ungrouped data

For ungrouped data, compute the statistics directly from the observations. For a frequency table, use each value's frequency: \(\bar{x}=\sum f_i x_i/\sum f_i\), where \(f_i\) is the frequency of value \(x_i\). For grouped intervals, the exact values are unknown; estimate the mean using class midpoints \(m_i\): \(\bar{x}\approx\sum f_i m_i/\sum f_i\). Locate the median from cumulative frequencies (middle rank) and the mode from the value/class with greatest frequency. Grouped results are estimates because the within-class values are not available.

4. Spread: range, variance, and standard deviation

Dispersion/variability describes how tightly values cluster or how widely they spread. Two data sets can have the same mean but very different variability.

  • Range = maximum − minimum. It is easy to compute but depends only on two values, so it is sensitive to extremes.
  • Variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Population variance: \(\sigma^2=\sum(x_i-\mu)^2/N\). Sample variance: \(s^2=\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2/(n-1)\). The sample denominator \(n-1\) is the usual unbiased estimator of population variance.
  • Standard deviation (SD) is the square root of variance: \(\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\), or \(s=\sqrt{s^2}\). It describes a typical distance from the mean in the original units. Small SD means values tend to lie close to the mean; large SD means they are more dispersed.

SD cannot be negative; its smallest possible value is 0 when all observations are identical. Because it uses distances from the mean, it is affected by outliers. It has the same units as the measurements; variance has squared units. SD is especially interpretable for reasonably symmetric distributions, but it is not itself a guarantee that data are normal.

Example: values 4, 6, 8 have mean 6. Their squared deviations sum to 8. Population variance is 8/3 and population SD is about 1.63. Sample variance is 8/2 = 4 and sample SD is 2. Know whether a question asks for sample or population calculation.

5. Distribution shape and visual summaries

A frequency table counts observations in values or intervals. A histogram plots numerical intervals (bins) on the horizontal axis and count or proportion on the vertical axis. The shape helps identify center, spread, gaps, clusters, skew, and possible unusual values. Bin choice affects appearance, so read the scale and bin widths.

  • Symmetric: left and right sides are approximately mirror images; mean and median are often similar.
  • Right-skewed (positive skew): a long tail extends to larger values; a few large observations commonly pull the mean rightward.
  • Left-skewed (negative skew): a long tail extends to smaller values; a few small observations commonly pull the mean leftward.

The direction of skew names the tail, not where most observations lie. The slide deck's written explanation on slides 42–43 reverses the positive/negative label while describing a right/left tail. Use the standard convention above: positive skew = right tail; negative skew = left tail.

A box plot (Tukey box-and-whisker plot) gives a compact view of a numerical distribution. The box spans Q1 to Q3; the line is the median; the interquartile range is IQR = Q3 − Q1. Whiskers extend according to the plotting convention. A common outlier rule flags values below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR for investigation; the points are not automatically errors. Box plots help compare centers, spreads, skew, and potential outliers across groups.

Skewness describes asymmetry; kurtosis describes aspects of tail weight/peakedness relative to a reference distribution. The slides name both as shape statistics but give no detailed kurtosis formula. For introductory interpretation, use histogram/box-plot shape and avoid treating a single statistic as a full description.

6. A practical EDA and summarization sequence

  1. Identify the observational unit, variables, types, units, and source.
  2. Inspect records and data quality: missing values, duplicates, impossible values, and unusual observations.
  3. Summarize categorical variables with counts/proportions; summarize numerical variables with center and spread.
  4. Plot distributions (e.g., histograms/box plots) and relationships; investigate patterns and anomalies.
  5. Use findings to decide on cleaning, transformations, and suitable modeling approaches.

Pivot tables, frequency tables, charts, subtotals, and totals are practical tools for summarization. The goal is a concise but faithful picture of the data, not a chart for every column.

Module 2 - Data Preparation

1. Purpose and anomalies

Data preparation (preprocessing) transforms raw data into a form suitable for analysis or modeling. Poor preparation can bias estimates, introduce errors, distort distributions, or prevent an algorithm from working. Common issues include missing values, outliers, inconsistent values/formats, duplicate records, incompatible schemas, inappropriate data types, limited predictors, and variables needing feature engineering. Preparation decisions should reflect the question and preserve information that matters.

2. Cleaning data

Missing values

First detect missing markers such as null, NaN, or blank strings. Determine whether missingness is accidental, structural (not applicable), or systematic. Options include removing rows/columns, filling numerical values with a mean or median, filling categories with a mode or explicit “unknown,” or using a more advanced imputation method. Mean imputation is sensitive to outliers; median is more robust. Deleting cases can shrink the sample and introduce bias if missingness is systematic. Choose a method based on missingness, variable type, and analysis goal, document it, and apply it consistently. Never treat a true zero as missing without evidence.

Duplicates, outliers, and inconsistent categories

Find duplicate rows or repeated entities using suitable keys. Remove redundant copies, or merge/aggregate when they represent valid separate events; for example, two purchases by the same customer are not duplicates just because the customer ID repeats. Outliers may be errors or genuine rare events. Detect using plots, IQR rules, or standardized scores (z-scores), then investigate and decide whether to retain, correct, remove, or cap/winsorize them. Apply decisions consistently and document them. Categorical inconsistencies can arise from spelling, case, and spaces (“F”, “female”, “FEMALE”); inspect unique values, normalize text, map equivalent labels, and validate the result.

Data types, dates, and scaling

Check that integers, decimals, strings, and dates are stored with the right types. Convert and validate: a text date may need parsing before chronological operations; an ID should usually remain categorical/text even when numeric-looking. Normalize date formats (the deck uses YYYY-MM-DD), identify time zones if relevant, and derive features like year/month/weekday only when they answer the question.

Min-max normalization maps numerical values to a bounded interval, often 0–1: \(x'=(x-x_{min})/(x_{max}-x_{min})\). Z-score standardization centers and scales using \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) (or sample statistics): values are expressed in standard deviations from the mean. Scaling prevents variables with large numerical units from dominating scale-sensitive methods. It is not always needed for tree-based methods. Fit scaling parameters on training data only, then reuse them for validation/test or future data, to avoid leakage.

Encoding categorical features

Models often require numeric inputs. Label encoding assigns category codes; it is appropriate for ordered categories if codes preserve the order, but arbitrary codes for nominal categories can falsely imply rank or distance. One-hot encoding creates an indicator column per category (often dropping a reference category in regression to avoid perfect collinearity). Choose based on variable meaning and model. Handle unseen categories at prediction time.

3. Integration: combining sources correctly

Integration joins information from different tables/systems. First align schemas: equivalent fields such as Cust_ID and CustomerID should be mapped to a common name and compatible type. Entity resolution determines whether records in separate datasets refer to the same real entity. Match on reliable keys (email, phone, IDs) where appropriate; when keys are absent or imperfect, use carefully validated fuzzy matching. A false match can be as harmful as a missed match.

Merging/joining combines columns using a shared key. An inner join keeps matching keys; a left join keeps all rows from the left table and matching right rows; a right join does the reverse; a full outer join keeps keys appearing on either side. Check row counts and unmatched keys after the join, especially for one-to-many relationships that can multiply rows. Concatenation appends rows (vertical combination) when schemas align. Before integration, convert units, currency, formats, and scales to compatible standards; record any conversion assumptions (e.g., exchange rate and date).

4. Data reduction and discretization

Data reduction produces a smaller representation while preserving information important to the analysis. Methods in the deck include:

  • Sampling: select a subset. Random sampling gives each case a chance of selection; stratified sampling preserves representation of important subgroups. A tiny sample may be unrepresentative.
  • Numerosity reduction by aggregation: replace detailed records with sums, counts, or averages (e.g., daily sales to monthly totals). This saves space but removes detail, so retain raw data if later questions need it.
  • Data-cube aggregation: summarize measures across multiple dimensions, such as sales by product, region, and time. Aggregation dimensions must match business questions.
  • Discretization/binning: turn continuous values into categories/intervals. Equal-width divides the numeric range into bins of equal size; equal-frequency/quantile aims for a similar number of records in each bin; custom/domain-based bins use meaningful business or subject-matter cutoffs. Equal-width can have uneven counts; equal-frequency has variable widths and ties may complicate equal counts. Binning simplifies interpretation but loses within-bin detail; state boundaries clearly.

5. A reliable preparation workflow

Profile each source, decide the grain (what one row represents), define keys and units, clean missing/duplicate/invalid data, resolve entities and align schemas, combine sources, encode/scale/transform as needed, reduce only if justified, then validate. Keep a reproducible record of rules and fit data-dependent transformations on training data only. After each step check row counts, data types, value ranges, missingness, and examples to confirm the change did what you intended.

Module 3 - Introduction to Regression Analysis

1. What regression does-and does not do

Regression analysis models the average relationship between a response/outcome (dependent variable, usually y) and one or more explanatory/predictor variables (independent variables, usually x). It can estimate associations, quantify conditional relationships, and predict outcomes for specified predictor values. A useful workflow starts with a business question, prepares and explores data, splits data, fits and compares candidate models, checks assumptions and residuals, evaluates on unseen data, and reports limitations and practical implications.

Regression estimates association; it does not by itself establish that a predictor caused the response. Confounding, omitted variables, selection, and reverse direction can explain associations. Correlation is also not causation. Use domain knowledge and an appropriate study design before making causal claims.

2. Simple and multiple linear regression

Simple linear regression has one predictor. The fitted line is:

\[\hat y=a+bx=\beta_0+\beta_1x\]

The intercept a/β0 is the predicted y when x=0; this may not be meaningful if x=0 is outside the data or impossible. The slope b/β1 is the predicted average change in y for a one-unit increase in x. State units and range: if x is thousands of dollars, one unit is $1,000.

The least-squares line chooses coefficients to minimize the sum of squared residuals. With paired observations (xᵢ,yᵢ), \(b=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)/\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\), and \(a=\bar y-b\bar x\). The predictions lie on the line; actual observations generally scatter around it.

Worked example from the slides: spend x = 1,000, 1,500, …, 4,000 corresponds to sales y = 8,000, 9,500, …, 17,000. Each $500 increase in spend accompanies $1,500 increase in sales, giving slope b=3 sales dollars per spend dollar; at x=1,000, 8,000= a+3(1,000), so a=5,000. The fitted line is ŷ=5,000+3x. At x=4,500, ŷ=$18,500. This prediction extends slightly beyond the observed predictor range, so it is extrapolation and should be treated cautiously.

Multiple linear regression uses several predictors: \(\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\cdots+\beta_px_p\). Holding the other included predictors fixed, βj is the model's estimated change in average y for a one-unit increase in xj. This “holding other variables fixed” interpretation is conditional, not proof of causation. For a house-price model, the predictors might be square footage, bedrooms, and distance from downtown. Standardized coefficients can help compare predictors measured in different units, though they do not measure causality or business value directly.

Nonlinear patterns may require transformed predictors, polynomial terms, or a nonlinear model. A straight-line model is inappropriate if the relationship bends materially. Do not interpret a polynomial coefficient as a simple one-unit effect without considering the full equation.

3. Correlation and predictor relationships

The correlation coefficient r measures direction and strength of a linear association and ranges from −1 to +1. Values near +1 indicate strong positive linear association; near −1 strong negative linear association; near 0 little linear association. A value near zero does not rule out a nonlinear relationship. Correlation is symmetric and does not designate predictor versus outcome. Scatterplots should accompany correlation: they reveal outliers, curvature, clusters, and changing spread.

Related predictors create multicollinearity. It can make coefficient estimates unstable, inflate standard errors, and make individual predictor effects hard to distinguish even if overall predictions remain reasonable. Inspect predictor correlations and use diagnostics such as variance inflation factor (VIF). Consider whether redundant variables can be removed or combined, guided by subject matter; do not mechanically delete important confounders just to improve a statistic.

4. Model preparation, fitting, and validation

Each row must represent a consistent observation (e.g., one campaign or one month). Check types and units, missing/duplicate/invalid values, extreme points, category encoding, and variable definitions. Explore predictor-outcome plots and correlations before fitting. Small samples can yield unstable estimates and large standard errors; too many predictors for the available observations increases risk.

Separate data into training, validation (if used for tuning), and test portions. The training data fit coefficients; validation compares/tunes candidate models; the test set provides a final estimate of performance on unseen observations. The deck's introductory example uses 80% training and 20% test. Do not choose a model based on test performance repeatedly; keep the test set for final assessment. For time-ordered data, split chronologically rather than randomly when future observations are the intended use.

Compare a simple model with added predictors or complexity. Prefer added complexity only when it improves test-set performance enough to justify it, coefficients make sense, and diagnostics are acceptable. A model can fit training data but perform poorly on unseen data (overfitting). A model too simple to capture the real pattern (underfitting) performs poorly on both training and test data.

5. Residuals, assumptions, and diagnostics

A residual is observed minus predicted: \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\). Positive residual means the model underpredicted; negative means it overpredicted. Plot residuals against fitted values or predictors. A suitable model should show residuals scattered randomly around zero, without a curve, trend, or many extreme points, and with roughly constant spread.

Core linear-model conditions to understand:

  • Linearity: the mean response changes linearly with the predictors as specified. Curved residual pattern suggests missed nonlinear structure; consider transformations or polynomial terms.
  • Independence: errors are not systematically dependent. Time, location, or repeated measurements can violate it.
  • Homoscedasticity: residual variance is approximately constant across fitted values. A fan/cone pattern indicates heteroscedasticity; transformations (e.g., log outcome when appropriate) or weighted least squares may help.
  • Residual normality: important for exact small-sample inference (tests/intervals); inspect a histogram or Q-Q plot. It is not a requirement that the predictors or outcome themselves be normally distributed.
  • Limited multicollinearity: predictors should not be near-linear combinations when individual coefficients need interpretation.

Diagnostics indicate where the model may be inadequate; they are not a box-checking ritual. The deck illustrates a residual cloud, curvature, and fan-shaped variance. A dot on the fitted line has residual zero; vertical distance from line/plane is prediction error.

6. Performance, significance, and interpretation

Use an independent test set to report predictive performance. Common regression metrics (know what they mean if included in class examples) are:

  • MAE = average absolute error; in outcome units and less sensitive to large errors than squared-error metrics.
  • MSE = average squared error; penalizes large errors strongly and has squared units.
  • RMSE = square root of MSE; in outcome units and still gives extra weight to large errors.
  • R² = proportion of observed outcome variation explained by the fitted model relative to a mean-only baseline, in the evaluation data. In-sample R² generally cannot decrease when predictors are added, so it alone is not a good model-selection rule and does not prove causality.

Statistical significance asks whether data provide evidence a coefficient differs from zero under model assumptions; a small p-value (often judged against a stated threshold such as 0.05) is not the probability the null is true or proof of a meaningful effect. Practical significance asks whether the effect size matters for decisions, costs, or expected value. A statistically significant but tiny coefficient may have little business impact. Report coefficients with units, uncertainty where available, test performance, assumptions/limitations, and context.

Omitted-variable bias can arise when an excluded relevant factor is associated with both the predictor and outcome; the observed two-variable association can differ in magnitude or direction from the conditional relationship. Include predictors for substantive reasons, not solely because they are available.

Extrapolation predicts at predictor values outside the observed range. The fitted relationship may not continue there, so flag it and avoid unwarranted confidence. A good business report answers the decision question, states model/data and performance, interprets key effects in units, identifies limitations, and gives a recommendation grounded in both model output and judgment.

  8. Predictors contain overlapping information. Coefficients can be unstable, individual effects hard to separate, and standard errors inflated.
  9. Likely overfitting: much stronger training than test performance. Choose using validation/training for tuning and report one-time test performance; prefer simpler models unless added complexity improves unseen performance and remains interpretable/diagnostically sound.
  10. Statistical significance is evidence against a zero coefficient under assumptions; practical significance is decision-relevant magnitude/value. Beyond the observed range, the relationship may change, so prediction relies on unsupported extrapolation.

Module 4 - Time Series and Forecasting

1. What is time-series analysis?

A time series is a sequence of observations measured at regular time intervals (hourly, daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, yearly, etc.). Time-series analysis examines how a variable behaves over time and uses past observations and sometimes past forecast errors to identify patterns and forecast future values. Forecasts support decisions such as staffing, inventory, budgeting, and planning; a forecast is an estimate, not a guaranteed future value.

Before modeling, define the decision, variable, forecast horizon, time unit, and acceptable error. Prepare data in chronological order with consistent intervals. Check missing periods/values, duplicates, date types, and unusual events such as promotions or emergencies. Plot the series and keep the most recent period for testing. Never randomly shuffle time-series observations when evaluating future forecasts: that can leak future information into training.

2. Components and patterns

  • Level: the typical baseline value around which observations vary.
  • Trend: sustained long-term increase or decrease, possibly due to population, technology, competition, or changing preferences.
  • Seasonality: recurring pattern at a known regular period, such as higher pool sales in spring/summer or December retail demand. Seasonality can occur within a year too (e.g., daily traffic cycle).
  • Cycle: longer-term ups and downs without a fixed calendar period (often tied to economic/business cycles).
  • Irregular/noise: unpredictable variation not explained by the systematic components.

These components can be combined additively (seasonal effect roughly constant in size) or multiplicatively (seasonal effect proportional to series level). The deck's visual examples emphasize trend and seasonality. If there is no visible trend or seasonality and the level is stable, simple mean/naïve/smoothing methods may be suitable; a trend or seasonal model is needed when those patterns are present.

3. Baselines and simple averages

A naïve forecast uses the latest actual value as the next forecast: \(F_{t+1}=y_t\). It is a simple baseline. A complex method is useful only if it improves on a reasonable baseline for the intended horizon.

The mean method forecasts the average of all available past observations. As a new actual value arrives, update the mean. It is appropriate when there is no noticeable trend or seasonality, but it responds slowly to changing levels.

4. Moving averages

A simple moving average of order k, MA(k), averages the most recent k actual observations:

\[F_{t+1}=\frac{y_t+y_{t-1}+\cdots+y_{t-k+1}}{k}\]

When new data arrive, include the newest observation and discard the oldest in the window. For k=3, average the last three observations to forecast the next period.

Example: if the last three weeks are 5.2, 6.7, and 5.8 million, next week's MA(3) forecast is (5.2+6.7+5.8)/3 = 5.9 million. If actual next-week sales are 6.0 million, forecast error e=actual−forecast=0.1 million. When k is small, the forecast reacts quickly to recent changes and is less smooth; when k is large, it is smoother/slower. Large k can help with wide, infrequent fluctuations; small k can react to level shifts. A k matching seasonal period (4 quarters or 12 months) can smooth that periodic effect, but basic MA does not model trend/seasonality well and generally lags trends.

Weighted moving average (WMA) assigns unequal weights to observations, often larger weights to recent values. Weights should sum to 1: \(F_{t+1}=\sum w_i y_i\). More recent data matter more by design. Exponential moving average (EMA) also emphasizes recent observations, with weights decaying exponentially into the past; this makes it respond faster than a long simple average.

5. Exponential smoothing models

Simple exponential smoothing (SES)

SES updates the forecast by combining the latest actual with the previous forecast: \(F_{t+1}=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)F_t\), where 0≤α≤1. Larger α gives more weight to the latest observation and responds faster; smaller α smooths more and reacts slower. It is suitable for data with a relatively stable level and no systematic trend or seasonality. Example from the slide: if actual week 24 is $6.0M, its forecast is $5.9M, and α=.30, forecast for week 25 is .30(6.0)+.70(5.9)=$5.93M.

Holt's trend method

Use Holt's method for a changing level with a trend but no important repeating seasonal pattern. It updates a level estimate using α and a trend estimate using β, then projects level + h×trend for h periods ahead. One common form is \(L_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)(L_{t-1}+T_{t-1})\), \(T_t=\beta(L_t-L_{t-1})+(1-\beta)T_{t-1}\), and \(F_{t+h}=L_t+hT_t\). α controls level updates; β controls trend updates. The slides' example forecasts June at 128 units and estimates an increase of about 8 units per month. Use trend estimates cautiously for long horizons because trends can change.

Holt–Winters method

Use Holt–Winters when the series has level, trend, and recurring seasonality. It extends Holt by updating a seasonal component; it uses α for level, β for trend, and γ for seasonal updates. Seasonal period m is number of observations in one cycle (monthly data with yearly seasonality: m=12; quarterly: m=4; weekly annual cycle: often m=52). Additive seasonality fits roughly constant seasonal swings; multiplicative seasonality fits swings that grow with the level. For illustration, if a normal December forecast is 500 and December factor is 1.30, multiplicative seasonal forecast is 500×1.30=650. The seasonal factor means December is about 30% above the normal level.

6. Accuracy, residuals, comparison, and uncertainty

Forecast error is \(e_t=y_t-F_t\): actual minus forecast. Positive error means underforecast; negative means overforecast. Common measures include MAE (mean absolute error, typical absolute error in original units), MSE (mean squared error, heavily penalizes large errors), RMSE (square root of MSE, original units), and MAPE (mean absolute percentage error). MAPE is unstable/undefined when actuals are zero or near zero. Lower error usually indicates better accuracy, but compare methods on the same held-out period and consider the business cost of different error directions.

Residuals/errors should fluctuate randomly around zero, without trend or repeating seasonality, with roughly stable variability and few extreme errors. Patterns mean useful structure remains unmodeled. Compare models on a chronological test period; select a method with lower test error, sensible forecasts, pattern-free residuals, and fit to the decision/horizon. Tune parameters on training/validation data, not by repeatedly optimizing the test set. A forecast interval expresses uncertainty around the forecast; wider intervals call for flexible plans (e.g., on-call staff) rather than false precision.

Module 5 - Association Rule Mining and Modeling

1. Purpose and business framing

Association-rule mining discovers items that frequently appear together in transactions (market-basket analysis). A rule has form \(A\to B\): A is the antecedent (left side) and B the consequent (right side). Example: {Pasta, Pasta Sauce} → {Parmesan}. It describes co-occurrence, not a causal claim that A makes customers buy B. Business uses include cross-selling, bundles, shelf placement, coupons, catalog recommendations, targeted marketing, pricing, and inventory planning.

Define the question, unit of analysis, and period before mining. For a supermarket, one completed basket is one transaction. Prepare transaction IDs and product identifiers; remove cancelled records, standardize product names/SKUs, resolve duplicate line items, and choose the item level (SKU, brand, or category). Convert each basket to a set or binary vector indicating item present (1) or absent (0). Quantity may be converted to presence when the question is whether an item appeared.

Explore basket count/size, item frequencies, and pair counts. Very common products can produce trivial rules; very rare items can yield unstable rules. Association rules use co-occurrence, not ordinary correlation. Do not infer causality. Where possible, mine earlier transactions and validate on later ones because promotion-only patterns may not persist.

2. Frequent itemsets and the Apriori principle

An itemset is a collection of items. An itemset is frequent if its support meets a chosen minimum-support threshold. Apriori finds frequent itemsets level by level:

  1. Count each item (1-itemset) and keep those meeting minimum support.
  2. Join frequent k-itemsets to form candidate (k+1)-itemsets.
  3. Prune candidates if any required subset is infrequent; count remaining candidates and retain those meeting support.
  4. Repeat until no larger frequent sets remain, then generate rules from frequent sets.

Key property: if an itemset is infrequent, every larger itemset containing it must also be infrequent. This downward-closure (Apriori) property prunes work. Apriori is straightforward for teaching and small data but may generate many candidates on large/dense data.

Five-basket slide example: T001 {Bread, Milk, Eggs}; T002 {Pasta, Sauce, Parmesan}; T003 {Bread, Butter, Milk}; T004 {Pasta, Sauce}; T005 {Bread, Milk, Butter}. With minimum support 40% (2 of 5), Bread and Milk each have 3/5=60%; Butter, Pasta, and Sauce each have 2/5=40%; Eggs and Parmesan have 1/5=20% and are removed. Frequent pairs include {Bread, Milk}=3/5=60%, {Bread, Butter}=2/5=40%, {Milk, Butter}=2/5=40%, {Pasta, Sauce}=2/5=40%. The triple {Bread, Milk, Butter} appears twice (40%) and is frequent. A rule can split any frequent itemset into a nonempty antecedent and remaining consequent, then be filtered by metrics.

3. FP-Growth

FP-Growth avoids generating a large set of candidate itemsets. It counts items, removes infrequent ones, orders remaining items by global frequency, inserts ordered transactions into a compact FP-tree, and merges shared prefixes by increasing node counts. A header table links nodes for the same item. For each suffix item, it constructs a conditional pattern base (prefix paths ending in that item), builds a conditional FP-tree, and mines patterns recursively.

In the five-basket example, after removing Eggs and Parmesan and using a common order, the Bread→Milk→Butter transactions share a path, while Pasta→Sauce transactions share another. Conditional patterns recover {Bread, Milk, Butter} and {Pasta, Sauce}. Apriori and FP-Growth can produce the same frequent itemsets/rules; FP-Growth is often more efficient on large/dense data. The choice depends on scale, clarity, and available tools.

4. Three key rule metrics

For rule A→B over M transactions:

  • Support is reach:
    \[support(A\to B)=\frac{count(A\cup B)}{M}\]
    It is the fraction of all baskets containing both sides.
  • Confidence is conditional reliability:
    \[confidence(A\to B)=\frac{count(A\cup B)}{count(A)}=\frac{support(A\cup B)}{support(A)}\]
    It is the fraction of A baskets that also contain B.
  • Lift compares against B's baseline popularity:
    \[lift(A\to B)=\frac{confidence(A\to B)}{support(B)}\]
    Lift >1 indicates positive association beyond independence; lift=1 indicates no association beyond the baseline; lift<1 indicates co-occurrence below the independent expectation.

Metrics answer different questions: support = reach, confidence = reliability among antecedent baskets, lift = incremental association relative to B's popularity. No single threshold determines business value.

Worked example: Bread and Milk occur together in 3 of 5 baskets, Bread in 3, Milk in 3. Support=3/5=60%; confidence(Bread→Milk)=3/3=100%; lift=1.00/0.60=1.67. Note the reverse rule Milk→Bread has same support and confidence in this example because the counts happen to match; in general confidence depends on direction. The deck's five-basket example states Pasta→Sauce has 40% support, 100% confidence, and lift 2.50. Its high confidence means every Pasta basket in that example also includes Sauce, while lift compares this to Sauce's baseline prevalence.

5. Thresholds, validation, and commercial action

Set minimum support for adequate reach, minimum confidence for conditional reliability, and minimum lift for association beyond baseline. Thresholds depend on use case, transaction volume, and cost of action; the deck's 50% discussion is illustrative, not a universal law. Inspect candidate rules together with eligible basket counts, margin, stock, discount cost, and operational feasibility. A statistically strong rule may still be commercially weak.

Validate rules on future/held-out transactions. Compare support, confidence, lift, stability by store/time, and estimated business value. Inspect example baskets and check whether the rule makes practical sense. A rule driven only by a short promotion can vanish when the promotion ends. If a bundle or shelf display is implemented, measure incremental margin or other business outcome; observed association alone does not show the intervention caused extra purchases.

Common pitfalls: claiming causality; mining too many weak rules; using thresholds without regard to reach; ignoring ubiquitous consequent items; failing to validate across time; treating duplicate/cancelled rows as valid baskets; and acting without checking stock, margins, and campaign costs.

6. Communicating findings

Report the business question, basket definition, transaction period, item level, thresholds, support/confidence/lift, validation period, and business action. State limitations and avoid causal language. Example: “In the discovery period, Pasta→Pasta Sauce had 40% support, 100% confidence, and lift 2.50. Test co-location in selected stores; assess incremental margin and inventory effects. This observed association does not prove the placement caused added demand.”
